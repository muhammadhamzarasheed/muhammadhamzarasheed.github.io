The person you are becoming

Muhammad Hamza · Assessment Lead · August 2026 · 4 minute read

Some time ago I came across the work of Pierre Bourdieu, and it reframed how I see luxury, taste, and the quiet race most of us never question. Bourdieu spent his career showing that what we admire, chase and call good taste is rarely natural or objective. It is a hierarchy built by those at the top, which works to protect their exclusivity, and rearranged the moment the rest of us begin to reach it.

He describes what happens when too many ordinary people gain access to a cultural form: it is quietly declared dead, not because it lost meaning, but because it stopped excluding. The hierarchy rebuilds itself one level further away. Watch the same mechanism in fashion today: logos give way to quiet luxury, and quiet luxury gives way to a minimalism only insiders can decode. The signals get subtler, and the circle of people who can read them gets smaller.

Nothing in this ranking is inherently superior. Classical music is not objectively better than pop, and one cuisine is not objectively better than another. The rankings feel real because they carry real social consequences, and they survive because we collectively keep believing in them.

The part that stays with me is where the rest of us fit. The middle of society is held in a permanent state of aspiration: chase the next brand, the next aesthetic, the next upgrade, and you will finally belong. But the horizon is designed to move. When the crowd approaches the symbol, the symbol is retired and a new one is minted. The chase resets.

The rarest luxury is stepping out of the race entirely.

Which brings me to a line from an old film that never left me: the saddest thing in life is wasted talent. I used to think wasted talent meant ability that never found success. I no longer believe that. The saddest thing is not failing. It is spending years wondering what might have happened if you had tried. It is talking yourself out of opportunities before giving yourself a chance, and letting fear, doubt or comfort make your decisions for you.

Very few people fall short because they lack talent. Most fall short because they underestimate what small, consistent actions become when they are repeated over time. A year feels long when you are looking ahead. It feels very short when you are looking back.

Potential means absolutely nothing until it is acted upon. And unlike the race for symbols, the work you put into yourself runs on a track nobody else owns. Nobody can retire the symbol, move the goalposts, or declare the form dead when too many people arrive.

The person you are becoming is the one arena where the rules cannot be rewritten without your consent. It is the one asset nobody else can revalue.

Muhammad Hamza · Manchester
